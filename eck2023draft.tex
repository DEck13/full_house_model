\begin{abstract}
The Full House Methodology is an approach for computing era-adjusted or system-adjusted statistics that balances the size of the eligible population in each system with the component's performance via "versus their peers" in a specific system. We assume that each component has a latent aptitude that can be calculated from this balancing act provided the system inclusion mechanism for components of the system is known. Without making any assumptions about the distribution of the system's components, the components' distribution is well-established from the nonparametric probability distribution.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Several academics have attempted to develop methods that can correct for era or system biases. The starting place for the answer to the problem of comparing players across eras can be found in the work of Stephen Jay Gould. \cite{Gould96} suggested that the entire distribution of achievement, or ``full house of variation,'' is relevant when considering the achievements of baseball players across eras. This idea was also recognized as the starting plac	e for an answer by \cite{SM13} and \cite{SM16}. 

\cite{BRL99, PPE11, SM13, SM16, PP20} provide several techniques to compare baseball players across eras, but they have fallen short in their objective and their results remain biased, as discussed in \cite{DE20} and \cite{DE20b}. In short, these previous attempts do not incorporate the evolving talent pool in calculations, which is necessary for understanding the changing distributions of achievement over time. The result is that existing methodologies produce a disproportionate number of players who began their career before baseball was integrated players in the rankings of baseball's greatest of all time. This era of baseball did not include a crucial population of eligible players. \cite{YZ16, meyer2021talents, campbell1990modeling} also develop methods for comparing components in historical popularity, educational attainment, and evaluation of employees. But they have similar problems as stated before and fail to account for the evolving talent pool.

In some circumstances, such as test equating, it is not required to take into account an evolving talent pool. \cite{andersson2013performing} offers a collaborative framework for test equating and builds the cumulative density function using the kernel method. Nevertheless, they are unable to resolve the extrapolation issue in non-parametric estimation, and the distribution's tail is not given special consideration.

Our Full House methodology directly incorporates the quality of the talent pool as a central modeling component. It offers metrics for assessing the performance of components in various eras or systems. With the application of educational attainment simulation, our Full House methodology reveals that the most talented students could perform worse than the other students who attend schools with lower mean scores. 

The next section establishes the model setting and specifies the assumptions in our Full House methodology. Section 3 considers an intriguing paradox that the component with more talent underperforms the component with less talent. We compute the probability of this paradox under different scenarios. Section 4 discusses the application of Full House methodology for the educational attainment simulation.

\section{Paradoxes Under Same Distribution}

Consider two populations $i = 1,2$ with sizes $N_1$ and $N_2$, and systems of sizes $n_1$ and $n_2$. We define a paradox (or a Gould paradox) when 
$$
  X_{1,(N_1 - k)} > X_{2,(N_2 - j)} \quad \text{and} \quad Y_{1,(n_1 - k)} < Y_{2,(n_2 - j)}
$$
both occur, where k, j $\in \mathbb{Z}$, $r(N_i, n_i, t) = N_i - n_i + t$, $i = 1,2$, $t = 1, \dots, n_i$. 

Or when 
$$
  X_{1,(k)} > X_{2,(j)} \quad \text{and} \quad Y_{1,(k)} < Y_{2,(j)}
$$
both occur, where k, j $\in \mathbb{Z}$, $r(N_i, n_i, t) = t$, $i = 1,2$, $t = 1, \dots, n_i$. 

A central interest is evaluating the probability of such a paradox, 

\begin{equation} \label{eq:simpleparadox}
  \Prob\left(X_{1,(N_1)} > X_{2,(N_2)}, Y_{1,(n_1)} < Y_{2,(n_2)}\right).
\end{equation}
As a simple illustrative example of some mechanics concerning paradoxes, suppose that $Y_{1,j} < Y_{2,k}$ a.s. Then \eqref{eq:simpleparadox} is equivalent to $\Prob\left(X_{1,(N_1)} > X_{2,(N_2)}\right)$, and we have $\Prob\left(X_{1,(N_1)} > X_{2,(N_2)}\right) \to 1$ as $N_1 \to \infty$. We motivate a convenient representation of \eqref{eq:simpleparadox} in the following theorem.


\begin{lemma} \label{lemma:rep}
Consider two populations $i = 1,2$ with sizes $N_1$ and $N_2$, and systems of sizes $n_1$ and $n_2$. 
Suppose that the system inclusion mechanism has the property that $g(X_{1,(p)}) = 1$ and $g(X_{2,(q)}) = 1$, and that the Full House Model is of the form 
\begin{align*}
  Y_{i,j} &\sim F_Y(y), \qquad i = 1,2, \qquad j = 1,\ldots,n_i, \\
  X_{i,j} &\sim F_X(x), \qquad i = 1,2, \qquad j = 1,\ldots,N_i, \\
\end{align*}
where $F_Y^{-1}(y)$ and $F_X^{-1}(x)$ exist. Then,

\begin{equation} \label{eq:pararep}
  \Prob\left(X_{1,(r(N_1, n_1, p))} > X_{2,(r(N_2, n_2, q)}, Y_{1,(p)} < Y_{2,(q)}\right) 
  = \Prob\left( X_{2,(r(N_2, n_2, q)} < X_{1,(r(N_1, n_1, p))}  < h_{1,2}\left(X_{2,(r(N_2, n_2, q)}\right)\right)
\end{equation}

where $h_{1,2}(\fatterdot) = (f_1^{-1} \circ f_2)(\fatterdot)$, 
$f_1(\fatterdot) = F_{Y_{1, (p)}}^{-1}\left(F_{X_{1,(r(N_1, n_1, p))}}\left(\fatterdot\right)\right)$, $f_2(\fatterdot) = F_{Y_{2, (q)}}^{-1}\left(F_{X_{2,(r(N_2, n_2, 1))}}\left(\fatterdot\right)\right)$,
and both $f$ and $h$ exist.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $f$ and $h$ exist, then we have
\begin{align*}
  &\Prob\left(X_{1,(N_1, n_1, p)} > X_{2,(r(N_2, n_2, q))}, Y_{1,(p)} < Y_{2,(q)}\right) \\
  &\qquad= \Prob\left(X_{1,(N_1, n_1, p)} > X_{2,(r(N_2, n_2, q))}, f_{1}(X_{1,(N_1, n_1, p)}) < f_{2}(X_{2,(r(N_2, n_2, q))})\right) \\
  &\qquad= \Prob\left( X_{2,(r(N_2, n_2, q))} < X_{1,(N_1, n_1, p)}  < h_{1,2}\left(X_{2,(r(N_2, n_2, q))}\right)\right).
\end{align*}
Existance of $f_i$ follows directly from the existance of $F_Y^{-1}(y)$, and existance of $h_{i,j}$ follows directly from the existance of $f_i$ and the existance of $F_X^{-1}(x)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem} \label{theorem:samedistgeneral}
If we assume 
\begin{align*}
  Y_{i,j} &\sim F_Y(y), \qquad i = 1,2, \qquad j = 1,\ldots,n_1, \\
  X_{i,j} &\sim F_X(x), \qquad i = 1,2, \qquad j = 1,\ldots,N_i, \\
\end{align*}

\begin{align*}
	&\Prob\left(X_{1,(N_1-k)} > X_{2,(N_2-j)}, Y_{1,(n_1 - k)} < Y_{2,(n_2 - j)}\right) \\
  &= \sum_{p=n_1-k}^{n_1}\left(\begin{array}{l}
n_1 \\
p
\end{array}\right)\frac{B(n_2-j+p, n_1-p+j+1)}{B(n_2-j, j+1)} - \sum_{p=N_1-k}^{N_1}\left(\begin{array}{l}

\end{array}\right)\frac{B(N_2-j+p, N_1-p+j+1)}{B(N_2-j, j+1)}.
\end{align*}

p
\end{array}\right)\frac{B(j+p, n_1 + n_2-p-j+1)}{B(j, n_2 -j+1)} - \sum_{p=k}^{N_1}\left(\begin{array}{l}

	&\Prob\left(X_{1,(N_1 - k)} > X_{2,(N_2 - j)}, Y_{1,(n_1 - k)} < Y_{2,(n_2 - j)}\right) = \Prob\left( X_{2,(N_2 - j)} < X_{1,(N_1 - k)}  < h_{1,2}\left(X_{2,(N_2 - j)}\right)\right) \\
	&= \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left[\int_{x}^{F_X^{-1} \circ g_{N_1,k}^{-1}  \circ g_{n_1, k} \circ g_{n_2,j}^{-1} \circ g_{N_2,j} \circ F_X(x)} f_{X_{1,(N_1 - k)}}(y)\ dy\right] \ f_{X_{2,(N_2 - j)}}(x) \ dx \\
	&= \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left[\int_{x}^{F_X^{-1} \circ g_{N_1,k}^{-1} \circ g_{n_1, k} \circ g_{n_2,j}^{-1} \circ g_{N_2,j} \circ F_X(x)} d \ g_{N_1, k}(F_X(y))\right] \ f_{X_{2,(N_2 - j)}}(x) \ dx \\
	&= \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left[g_{n_1, k} \circ g_{n_2, j}^{-1} \circ g_{N_2, j} \circ F_X(x) - g_{N_1, k} \circ F_X(x)\right] \ f_{X_{2,(N_2 - j)}}(x) \ dx \\
	&= \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left[g_{n_1, k} \circ g_{n_2, j}^{-1} \circ g_{N_2, j} \circ F_X(x) - g_{N_1, k} \circ F_X(x)\right] \  d \ g_{N_2, j}(F_X(x))\\
	&= \int_0^1 g_{n_1, k} \circ g_{n_2, j}^{-1}(t) - g_{N_1, k} \circ g_{N_2, j}^{-1}(t) \ dt\\
	&= \int_0^1 g_{n_1, k}(x) \ dg_{n_2, j}(x)- \int_0^1g_{N_1, k}(x) \ dg_{N_2, j}(x)\\
	&= \int_0^1 I_x(k, n_1 - k+1) \frac{x^{j-1}(1-x)^{n_2 - j}}{B(j, n_2 - j+1)} \ dx - \int_0^1 I_x(k, N_1 - k+1) \frac{x^{j-1}(1-x)^{N_2 - j}}{B(j, N_2 - j+1)} \ dx\\
	&= \int_0^1 \sum_{p=k}^{n_1}\left(\begin{array}{l}
n_1 \\
p
\end{array}\right) x^p(1-x)^{n_1-p} \frac{x^{j-1}(1-x)^{n_2 - j}}{B(j, n_2 - j+1)} \ dx \\
&- \int_0^1 \sum_{p=k}^{N_1}\left(\begin{array}{l}
N_1 \\
p
\end{array}\right) x^p(1-x)^{N_1-p} \frac{x^{j-1}(1-x)^{N_2 -j}}{B(j, N_2-j+1)} \ dx\\ 
&= \sum_{p=k}^{n_1}\left(\begin{array}{l}
n_1 \\
p
\end{array}\right)\frac{B(j+p, n_1 + n_2-p-j+1)}{B(j, n_2-j+1)} - \sum_{p=k}^{N_1}\left(\begin{array}{l}
N_1 \\
p
\end{array}\right)\frac{B(j+p, N_1+N_2-p-j+1)}{B(j, N_2-j+1)}
\end{align*}
where 
\begin{align*}
	h_{1,2}(x) &= (g_1 \circ f_2)(x) \\
	&= F_X^{-1} \circ g_{N_1,k}^{-1} \circ g_{n_1, k} \circ g_{n_2,j}^{-1} \circ g_{N_2,j} \circ F_X(x) \\
	g_{n,i}(x) &= I_x(i, n-i+1) \\
\end{align*}

and $I_x(\alpha, \beta)$ is the regularized incompleted beta function. 

\end{proof}

\begin{defn}
We will say that $Y_2$ is first order $\varepsilon$-weakly stochastically dominant over $Y_1$ if, for $\varepsilon > 0$, 
$
  \Prob(Y_2 \leq y) \leq \Prob(Y_1 \leq y) \leq \Prob(Y_2 \leq y) + \varepsilon
$
for all $y$ and 
$
  \Prob(Y_2 \leq y) < \Prob(Y_1 \leq y) \leq \Prob(Y_2 \leq y) + \varepsilon
$
for some $y$.
\end{defn}


\begin{prop} \label{prop:weakstodom}
Suppose that the system inclusion mechanism has the property that $g(X_{i,(N_i)}, \textbf{X}_{i,-(N_i)}) = 1$, and that the Full House Model is of the form 
\begin{align*}
  Y_{i,j} &\sim F_{Y_i}(y), \qquad i = 1,2, \qquad j = 1,\ldots,n_i, \\
  X_{i,j} &\sim F_X(x), \qquad i = 1,2, \qquad j = 1,\ldots,N_i, \\
\end{align*}
where $F_{Y_i}^{-1}(y)$ and $F_X^{-1}(x)$ exist. Further suppose that $Y_2$ is first order $\varepsilon$-weakly stochastically dominant over $Y_1$ for some $\varepsilon > 0$. Then,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:paradox2}
\begin{split}
  &\frac{n_2}{n_1 + n_2} - \frac{N_2}{N_1 + N_2} \leq \Prob\left(X_{1,(N_1)} > X_{2,(N_2)}, Y_{1,(n_1)} < Y_{2,(n_2)}\right) \\
  &\qquad\leq n_2\sum_{i=0}^{n_1} \frac{{n_1 \choose i}\varepsilon^i}{n_1+n_2-i}- \frac{N_2}{N_1 + N_2}
  %\frac{n_2}{n_1 + n_2} + \varepsilon \frac{n_1n_2}{n_1 + n_2 - 1} - \frac{N_2}{N_1 + N_2}  + o(\varepsilon).
\end{split}
\end{equation}
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
First, observe that the assumption of first order $\varepsilon$-weakly stochastically dominant gives 
$$
  \left(F_{Y_{2,(n_2)}}(y)\right)^{n_1/n_2} \leq \left(F_{Y_1}(y)\right)^{n_1} \leq \left(F_{Y_2}(y) + \varepsilon\right)^{n_1} = \left(\left(F_{Y_{2,(n_2)}}(y)\right)^{1/n_2} + \varepsilon\right)^{n_1},
$$
and this implies that 
$$
   F_X^{-1}\left(F_X^{\frac{N_2}{N_1}\frac{n_1}{n_2}}(X_{2,(N_2)})\right) \leq

We verify the normal example with a Monte Carlo simulation. In each simulation, we first randomly generate two groups of observation: $Y_1$ and $Y_2$ from the normal distribution with $\mu_1 = -1$ and $\mu_2 = 1$, and the number of each group is given by $n_1$ and $n_2$. Based on our setting, we assume the latent aptitudes follow the Pareto distribution with $\alpha = 1.16$, $N_1 = 1e5$ and $N_2 = 2e5$. Then we obtain the latent aptitudes for each group and check whether the paradox happens. We repeat this process 1000 times and record the frequency of the Gould paradox happening. 1000 Monte Carlo iterations of our simulation are performed and we calculate the average frequency divided by 1000 as the probability of the Gould paradox happening. Next, using the procedure in \cite{mi2009mvtnorm}, we calculate the probability of our outcome, which involves the probability of a multivariate normal distribution.

\subsection{Simulation of Students in Different Educational Stages}

In this simulation, we simulate the process of students moving on to the next level of education from primary school to the university in an ideal setting. We consider the student's achievement as the only criterion for school admission. First, we randomly generate 1 million talents from Pareto distribution, which corresponds to the 1 million talents of primary school students. Then we randomly generate 2 thousand school indices from 1 to 2000 and assign them to the students we generated before, which represent the schools that the students will attend. Finally, we transform ordered talents corresponding to the eligible population to percentiles from the ordered statistics and convert percentiles to ordered scores from a normal distribution, which could represent the scores the students achieve in the schools.  The final step can be described in the following formula: \begin{equation} \label{eq:estiY}	Y_{i,(j)} = F_{Y_{i}}^{-1} \left( F_{X_{i}}\left(X_{i, (500*(i-1) + j))} \right) \right)\end{equation}

$$
\begin{aligned}
F_{X_i}\left(X_{i,(j)} \right) & \sim U_{i,(j)}, 
F_{X_{i,(j)}}\left(X_{i,(j)} \right) & \sim U_{i,j}
\end{aligned}
$$
where 
$\sim$ means distributed as, $U_{i,j} \sim U(0,1)$, and $U_{i,(j)} \sim \operatorname{Beta}(j, n+1-j)$, n is the sample size. $X_{i, (j)}$ is the jth ordered talent in the ith school. $F_{X_{i}}$ is the cumulative distribution function of talents in the ith school. $Y_{i, (j)}$ is the jth ordered score in the ith school. $F_{Y_{i}}$ is the cumulative distribution function of scores in the ith school. The score distribution for each school follows the Normal distribution with different parameters. We assume the mean of the Normal distribution ranges from 60 to 80 with equal space, which indicates the different levels of schools. Better schools have higher mean scores. The standard deviation for Figure \ref{Fig:education1} is 2 and Figure \ref{Fig:education2} is 5. Students who achieve in the top 500 will advance to the next level of the best school, then students who achieve in the top 501 to 1000 will advance to the next level of the 2nd best school, and the same with the rest.50 Monte Carlo iterations of our simulation are performed. The tendencies of the most talented students in each of the 50 simulations are displayed in the following figures. The primary\_index0 in the x-axis indicates the initial schools for the students with the highest talent. The levels of schools are represented by the index on the y-axis, which goes from 1 to 2000. High-achieving schools are a sign of superiority. The following are the schools that students will attend: primary\_index1 through primary\_index3. It is evident that even if gifted children begin their education at a lesser school, they may advance to a top institution within two to three stages.

\subsection{Gould Paradox in Major League Baseball}

In this analysis, we compute the probability of the paradox using the results from the Normal distribution example above and illustrate it in the application of Major League Baseball (MLB) since it is widely acknowledged that the batting average follows Normal distribution. Specifically, we compute the probability that the top batting average in 2023 is lower than the top batting average in the other season, but the players who have the highest batting average in 2023 are more talented than those who have the highest batting average in the other season. 

In this analysis, we detail the considerations made for the batting average statistics that we era-adjust using the Full House Model \citep{DE20b}. Park factor adjustment is considered in this analysis and we apply the adjusted park index from \cite{SM16} to all ballparks from the 1871 season to the 2022 season. The batting average can be affected by the ballpark and we apply the park factor adjustment to this statistic, which accounts for the handedness of batters.

The Full House Model will only be applied to the statistics obtained by full-time players. We define the full-time hitter cutoff as the median PAs after screening out hitters who appeared in fewer than 75 PAs. This criterion is flexible enough to account for changing the number of games played over time as well as seasons shortened by labor strikes and pandemics.

In the 1994 and 1995\footnote{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1994\%E2\%80\%9395\_Major\_League\_Baseball\_strike}, hitters and pitchers played fewer games per season than the regular season. To deal with extreme statistics from the small sample size, we employed a shrinkage method that adjusted the raw statistics toward a global average. Our shrinkage method follows a shrinkage of ballpark effect estimates motivated from \cite{SM16}. The details can be found in \cite{DE20b}.

We compute the talent scores for the batting average in each season using the (\ref{eq:trueparX}). Then the theoretical results from the Normal distribution example above can be applied to this example and compute the paradox probability of all the seasons from 1871 to 2022 compared with 2023. Fig \ref{Fig:AVG_prob} displays the tendency of the paradox probability of all the seasons from 1871 to 2022 compared with 2023.
