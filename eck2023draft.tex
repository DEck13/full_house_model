\section{Introduction}

Full House Modeling provides a novel foundation for comparative analysis of individuals evaluated across differing contexts. This foundation originated in an analysis comparing baseball players across eras \citep{yan2025fullhouse}. The historical significance of this analysis was further studied and presented in \cite{burgos2025change}. In baseball these analyses are game changing and gaining traction in the broader baseball analytics community. 
Full House Modeling was expanded upon in an investigation of the impact of historical figures \citep{cohen2025contextualizing}. \textbf{needs to be edited and made much more interesting}

In this article we formalize Full House Methodology. Under this formalization we study probabilities of observing paradoxes between latent aptitude and observed achievement in comparative analyses between individuals being compared across contexts. Here, the paradox is that an individual from one context can have higher aptitude and simultaneous lower observed achievement than an individual from another context. We provide explicit probabilities of these paradoxes in two settings: 1) when observed achievement is independent and identically distributed (iid); 2) when the observed statistics across contexts follow different normal distributions but are still independent. We also study bounds to the paradoxes in more general settings. We also demonstrate that when observed achievement is normally distributed, the paradox probabilities can be reliably estimated via plug-in.  


\section{Model Setting}

We now motivate the structure of the Full House Model. Let $N_i$ denote the size of population $i$. We will suppose the every individual $k = 1,\ldots, N_i$ has an underlying latent $\R$-valued aptitude 
$X_{i,1}, \ldots, X_{i,N_i} \overset{iid}{\sim} F_{X}$. Let $\textbf{X}_{i,-j}$ be the vector of all latent aptitudes not including the component $j$. We then define an indicator function $1_{i}\left(X_{i,j},\textbf{X}_{i,-j}\right)$ as a determined and known system inclusion function for population $i$ where a 1 indicates inclusion and a 0 indicates exclusion. 
%$g_{i}\left(X_{i,j}, \textbf{X}_{i,-j}\right)=1$ indicates that individual $j$ is included in the $i$th system, and $g_{i}\left(X_{i,j}, \textbf{X}_{i,-j}\right)=0$ indicates that individual $j$ is not included in the $i$th system. 
We now suppose that an individual $j$ included in system $i$ has an observed $\R$-valued statistic $Y_{i,j} \overset{iid}{\sim} F_{Y_i}$, $j = 1,\ldots,n_i$, $n_i \leq N_i$. As we will see later, the observed $Y_{i,j}$ values are one-to-one realizations of $X_{i,j}$, provided that $g_{i}\left(X_{i,j}, \textbf{X}_{i,-j}\right)=1$. Therefore $Y_{i,1}, \ldots, Y_{i,n_i} \overset{iid}{\sim} F_{Y_i}$. 

Let $X_{i,(j)}$ and $Y_{i,(j)}$ denote the $j$th order statistic for system $i$. Here, larger numbers will correspond to a notion of "better." Thus $X_{i,(j)} > X_{i,(k)}$ whenever $j>k$, for example. We now form $(Y,X)$ pairs of random variables for each individual included in the system. These pairs are of the form $(Y_{i,(j)}, X_{i,(r_{i,j})})$ where $r_{i,j}$ indexes the aptitude of the $j$th highest value observed from the system. As an example, let $1_{i}\left(X_{i,j}, \textbf{X}_{i,-j}\right) = 1(X_{i,j} \geq X_{i,(N_i-n_i+1)})$, then the $(Y_{i,(j)}, X_{i,(r_{i,j})})$ pairs take the form $(Y_{i,(j)}, X_{i,(N_i - n_i + j)})$ for $j = 1,\ldots,n_i$.

%where we suppose that $Y_{i,1}, \ldots, Y_{i,n_i} \overset{iid}{\sim} F_{Y_i}$, $F_{Y_i}$ is continuous, and $n_i < N_i$ is the number of individuals included in system $i$. We denote $Y_{i,(j)}$ as the $j$th ordered statistic for system $i$.
The key Full House Modeling is the explicit connection between the elements forming the $(Y_{i,(j)}, X_{i,(r_{i,j})})$ pairs. Full House Modeling states this connection as
\begin{equation} \label{eq:truepar}
\begin{split}
  Y_{i, (j)} &= F_{Y_{i, (j)}}^{-1} \circ F_{X_{i, (r_{i,j})}} \left(X_{i, (r_{i,j})}\right), \\
  X_{i, (r_{i,j})} &= F_{X_{i, (r_{i,j})}}^{-1} \circ F_{Y_{i,(j)}} \left(Y_{i,(j)} \right),
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where $1_i(X_{i, (r_{i,j})}, \textbf{X}_{i, -(r_{i,j})}) = 1$, and both $F_{Y_i}$ and $F_X$ are continuous and invertible. We summarise Full House Modeling in the definition below.


\begin{defn} [Full House Model]
\label{def:fhm}
	Consider populations with sizes $N_i$ and system sizes $n_i$, $N_i \geq n_i$. Let a system inclusion function $1_i(\cdot,\cdot)$ be specified. Let $X_{i,j} \overset{iid}{\sim} F_X$, $j = 1,\ldots,N_i$. Let $Y_{i,j} \overset{iid}{\sim} F_{Y_i}$, $j = 1,\ldots, n_i$. Form pairs $(Y_{i,(j)}, X_{i,(r_{i,j})})$, $j = 1,\ldots,n_i$ for all individuals such that $1_i(X_{i, (r_{i,j})}, \textbf{X}_{i, -(r_{i,j})}) = 1$. Then the Full House Model generates $Y_{i,(j)}$ values following \eqref{eq:truepar} where $F_{Y_i}$ and $F_X$ are continuous and invertible.
\end{defn}

Note that Definition~\ref{def:fhm} fixes $F_X$ but allows $F_{Y_i}$ to vary across systems. 

\vspace{12pt}\textbf{Remarks}:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item The definition of a Full House Model immediately implies structural missingness. Every $X_{i,j}$ such that $1_i(X_{i,j},\textbf{X}_{i,-j}) = 0$ will not have a corresponding $Y$ value. In the following sections we will consider probabilities involving both $X$ and $Y$ quantities. To handle this structural missingness, we will suppose that $Y_{i,j}$ values that are not observed are not $\Prob$-measurable.
	%\item The probability integral transformation steps in \eqref{eq:truepar} involving $F_{X_{i, (r_{i,j})}} \left(X_{i, (r_{i,j})}\right)$ and $F_{Y_{i,(j)}} \left(Y_{i,(j)} \right)$ may be cumbersome to evaluate in practice. Instead one may replace $F_{X_{i, (r_{i,j})}} \left(X_{i, (r_{i,j})}\right)$ with $F_{U_{i,(r_{i,j})}} \circ F_X \left(X_{i, (r_{i,j})}\right)$ where $U_{i,(j)} \sim $ \,Beta$(r_{i,j},N_i-r_{i,j}+1)$, and may replace $F_{Y_{i,(j)}} \left(Y_{i,(j)} \right)$ with $F_{U_{i,(j)}} \circ F_{Y_i}\left(Y_{i,(j)} \right)$ where $U_{i,(j)} \sim $\, Beta$(j,n_i-j+1)$.
\end{enumerate}






\section{Paradoxes Under Identical Distributions}
\label{sec:samedist}

In this section we will provide explicit paradox probability results in a Full House Modeling setting involving two populations with the additional specification that $F_{Y_i} = F_{Y}$, $i=1,2$ in Definition~\ref{def:fhm}. The following definition formally defines the notion of paradox used throughout this article.

\begin{defn}\label{def:paradox}
Consider the Full House Model in Definition~\ref{def:fhm} with two populations denoted by $i=1,2$. We say a paradox has occurred when both $X_{1,(r_{1,j})} > X_{2,(r_{2,k})}$ and  $Y_{1,(j)} < Y_{2,(k)}$ have occurred. The paradox probability is defined as
\begin{equation} \label{eq:simpleparadox}
  \Prob\left(X_{1,(r_{1,j})} > X_{2,(r_{2,k})}, Y_{1,(j)} < Y_{2,(k)}\right).
\end{equation}	
\end{defn}

As a simple illustrative example of the above, let $r_{i,t} = N_i - n_i + t$ and set $j = n_1$ and $k = n_2$, and further suppose that $Y_{1,j} < Y_{2,k}$ a.s. Then the paradox probability \eqref{eq:simpleparadox} is equivalent to $\Prob\left(X_{1,(N_1)} > X_{2,(N_2)}\right)$, and we have $\Prob\left(X_{1,(N_1)} > X_{2,(N_2)}\right) \to 1$ when $N_1 \to \infty$. We now introduce a convenient representation which allows the paradox probability to be expressed in terms of the latent aptitudes alone.


\begin{lemma} \label{lemma:rep}
Consider the Full House Model in Definition~\ref{def:fhm} with two populations, where $F_{Y_i} = F_{Y}$ for $i=1,2$. Then, 
\begin{equation} \label{eq:pararep}
  \Prob\left(X_{1,(r_{1,j})} > X_{2,(r_{2,k})}, Y_{1,(j)} < Y_{2,(k)}\right) 
  = \Prob\left( X_{2,(r_{2,k})} < X_{1,(r_{1,j})}  < h_{1,2}\left(X_{2,(r_{2,k})}\right)\right),
\end{equation}
where $h_{1,2,j,k}(\cdot) = F_{X_{1,(r_{1,j})}}^{-1} \circ F_{Y_{1, (j)}} \circ F_{Y_{2, (k)}}^{-1} \circ F_{X_{2,(r_{2,k})}} (\cdot)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Define $f_{1,j}(\cdot) = F_{Y_{1, (j)}}^{-1} \circ F_{X_{1,(r_{1,j})}} (\cdot)$ and 
$f_{2,k}(\cdot) = F_{Y_{2, (k)}}^{-1} \circ F_{X_{2,(r_{2,k})}} (\cdot)$. Then we have 
\begin{align*}
  &\Prob\left(X_{1,(r_{1,j})} > X_{2,(r_{2,k})}, Y_{1,(j)} < Y_{2,(k)}\right) \\
  &\qquad= \Prob\left(X_{1,(r_{1,j})} > X_{2,(r_{2,k}))}, f_{1,j}(X_{1,(r_{1,j})}) < f_{2,k}(X_{2,(r_{2,k})})\right) \\
  &\qquad= \Prob\left( X_{2,(r_{2,k})} < X_{1,(r_{1,j})}  < h_{1,2}\left(X_{2,(r_{2,k})}\right)\right),
\end{align*}
where $h_{1,2,j,k}(\cdot) = (f_{1,j}^{-1} \circ f_{2,k})(\cdot)$. %Existence of $f_i$ follows directly from the existence of $F_Y^{-1}(y)$, and existence of $h_{i,j}$ follows directly from the existence of $f_i$ and the existence of $F_X^{-1}(x)$.
\end{proof}

Our first formal result provides conditions that allow for an explicit paradox probability result. Those conditions require the following definition.

\begin{defn}
\label{def:ribf}
Let $\alpha,\beta > 0$. Then the regularized incomplete beta function is defined as
$$
  I_{\alpha,\beta}(x) = \frac{1}{B(\alpha,\beta)} \int_0^x u^{\alpha-1}(1-u)^{\beta-1}du,
$$
where $B(\alpha,\beta) = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha + \beta)}$ is the beta coefficient.
\end{defn}

Note that the regularized incomplete beta function is the cumulative distribution function of a $\text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)$ distribution. 

\begin{theorem} \label{theorem:samedistgeneral}
Consider the Full House Model in Definition~\ref{def:fhm} with two populations, where $F_{Y_i} = F_{Y}$ for $i=1,2$. Denote by $I$ the regularized incomplete beta function. Further assume that
\begin{equation} \label{eq:technical-condition}
  I_{j, n_1-j+1} \circ I_{k, n_2-k+1}^{-1} (t) - I_{r_{1,j}, N_1 - r_{1,j}+1} \circ I_{r_{2,k}, N_2 - r_{2,k} + 1}^{-1}(t) \geq 0, \qquad \text{for all} \; t \in (0,1).
\end{equation}
Under these assumptions,
\begin{align*}
	&\Prob\left(X_{1,(r_{1,j})} > X_{2,(r_{2,k})}, Y_{1,(j)} < Y_{2,(k)}\right) \\
  &= \sum_{p=j}^{n_1}\left(\begin{array}{l}
n_1 \\
p
\end{array}\right)\frac{B(k+p, n_2-n_1-p+k+1)}{B(k, n_2-k+1)} - \sum_{p=r_{1,j}}^{N_1}\left(\begin{array}{l}
N_1 \\
p
\end{array}\right)\frac{B(r_{2,k}+p, N_2-N_1-p+r_{2,k}+1)}{B(r_{2,k}, N_2-r_{2,k}+1)}.
\end{align*}
\end{theorem}


\noindent \textbf{Remarks}:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item The result of Theorem~\ref{theorem:samedistgeneral} is distribution free. It only depends on the sample sizes $n_i, N_i$, $i = 1,2$, the system inclusion function, and the indexes of the order statistics under comparison. The distribution on $Y$ is eliminated by Lemma~\ref{lemma:rep}. To see this, consider the distribution $F_{Y_{(j)}}$ for some $j = 1,\ldots,n_1$. The random variable $Y_{(j)} = F_Y^{-1} \circ U_{(j)}$ where $U_{(j)} \sim \text{Beta}(j,n_1-j+1)$. Thus, $F_{Y_{(j)}}(y) = I_{j,n_1-j+1} \circ F_Y (y)$. With this in mind, from Lemma~\ref{lemma:rep} we have that
\begin{align*}
	h_{1,2,j,k}(x) &= F_{X_{1,(r_{1,j})}}^{-1} \circ F_{Y_{1, (j)}} \circ F_{Y_{2, (k)}}^{-1} \circ F_{X_{2,(r_{2,k})}} (x) \\
	&= F_{X_{1,(r_{1,j})}}^{-1} \circ I_{j,n_1-j+1} \circ \left(F_Y \circ F_Y^{-1}\right) \circ I^{-1}_{k,n_2-k+1} \circ F_{X_{2,(r_{2,k})}} (x) \\
	&= F_{X_{1,(r_{1,j})}}^{-1} \circ I_{j,n_1-j+1}  \circ I^{-1}_{k,n_2-k+1} \circ F_{X_{2,(r_{2,k})}} (x).
\end{align*}
The above is the upper-bound of an integral with respect to $X$s in the proof of Theorem~\ref{theorem:samedistgeneral}. The influence of the distributional specification on $Y$ does not influence the calculation of the paradox probability result in Theorem~\ref{theorem:samedistgeneral}.
	\item The distribution on $X$ is eliminated when calculating the paradox probability \eqref{eq:simpleparadox} in its Lemma~\ref{lemma:rep} representation, $\Prob\left( X_{2,(r_{2,k})} < X_{1,(r_{1,j})}  < h_{1,2}\left(X_{2,(r_{2,k})}\right)\right)$. These calculations involve double integrals with respect to densities for $F_{X_{1,(r_{1,j})}}$ and $F_{X_{2,(r_{2,k})}}$. One integral evaluation and a reparameterization remove the specification of $F_X$ from the probability calculation as seen in the proof of Theorem~\ref{theorem:samedistgeneral}.
	\item The steps mentioned in the last sentence of the previous remark allow the probability calculation for $\Prob\left( X_{2,(r_{2,k})} < X_{1,(r_{1,j})}  < h_{1,2}\left(X_{2,(r_{2,k})}\right)\right)$ to simplify to 
$$
	\int_0^1\left\{I_{j, n_1-j+1} \circ I_{k, n_2-k+1}^{-1} (t) - I_{r_{1,j}, N_1 - r_{1,j}+1} \circ I_{r_{2,k}, N_2 - r_{2,k} + 1}^{-1}(t)\right\}^+ dt.
$$
The technical condition \eqref{eq:technical-condition} ensures that this integral defines a non-negative probability in strong terms. We demonstrate with analytic and numeric arguments when \eqref{eq:technical-condition} is satisfied and the results of Theorem~\ref{theorem:samedistgeneral} therefore hold.
\end{enumerate}


\begin{proof}
First, Lemma~\ref{lemma:rep} allows us to write
$$
	\Prob\left(X_{1,(r_{1,j})} > X_{2,(r_{2,k})}, Y_{1,(j)} < Y_{2,(k)}\right) = \Prob\left( X_{2,(r_{2,k})} < X_{1,(r_{1,j})}  < h_{1,2,j,k}\left(X_{2,(r_{2,k})}\right)\right),
$$
where $h_{1,2,j,k}(\cdot) = F_{X_{1,(r_{1,j})}}^{-1} \circ F_{Y_{1, (j)}} \circ F_{Y_{2, (k)}}^{-1} \circ F_{X_{2,(r_{2,k})}} (\cdot)$. We can rewrite $h_{1,2,j,k}(\cdot)$ into a form that will allow for specific computation involving Beta distributions. o see this, consider the distribution $F_{Y_{(j)}}$ for some $j = 1,\ldots,n_1$. The random variable $Y_{(j)} = F_Y^{-1} \circ U_{(j)}$ where $U_{(j)} \sim \text{Beta}(j,n_1-j+1)$. Thus, $F_{Y_{(j)}}(y) = g_{n,j} \circ F_Y (y)$,
%\begin{align*}
%  P\left(X_{2,(r_{2,k})} < x\right) &= P\left(U_{(r_{2,k})} < F_X(x)\right) \\ 
%  &= \frac{1}{B(r_{2,k},N_{2}-r_{2,k}+1)}\int_0^{F_X(x)}u^{r_{2,k}-1}(1-u)^{N_2-r_{2,k}} du	\\
%  &= g_{N_2,r_{2,k}} \circ F_X (x),
%\end{align*}
where we write $g_{n,j}(x) = I_{j, n-j+1}(x)$ for ease of notation. This expression of distribution functions allows us to write 
\begin{align*}
	&\Prob\left( X_{2,(r_{2,k})} < X_{1,(r_{1,j})}  < h_{1,2,j,k}\left(X_{2,(r_{2,k})}\right)\right) \\
	&= \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left\{\int_{x}^{F_X^{-1} \circ g_{N_1,r_{1,j}}^{-1} \circ g_{n_1, j} \circ g_{n_2,k}^{-1} \circ g_{N_2,r_{2,k}} \circ F_X(x)} f_{X_{1,(r_{1,j})}}(y)\ dy\right\} \ f_{X_{2,(r_{2,k})}}(x) \ dx \\
	&= \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left\{\int_{x}^{F_X^{-1} \circ g_{N_1,r_{1,j}}^{-1} \circ g_{n_1, j} \circ g_{n_2,k}^{-1} \circ g_{N_2,r_{2,k}} \circ F_X(x)} d \ g_{N_1, r_{1,j}}(F_X(y))\right\} \ f_{X_{2,(r_{2,k})}}(x) \ dx \\
	&= \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left\{g_{n_1, j} \circ g_{n_2, k}^{-1} \circ g_{N_2, r_{2,k}} \circ F_X(x) - g_{N_1, r_{1,j}} \circ F_X(x)\right\}^+ \ f_{X_{2,(r_{2,k})}}(x) \ dx \\
	&= \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left\{g_{n_1, j} \circ g_{n_2, k}^{-1} \circ g_{N_2, r_{2,k}} \circ F_X(x) - g_{N_1, r_{1,j}} \circ F_X(x)\right\}^+ \  d \ g_{N_2, r_{2,k}}(F_X(x)) \\
	&= \int_0^1 \left\{g_{n_1, j} \circ g_{n_2, k}^{-1}(t) - g_{N_1, r_{1,j}} \circ g_{N_2, r_{2,k}}^{-1}(t)\right\}^+	 \ dt.
\end{align*}
Condition~\eqref{eq:technical-condition} states that 
$$
\int_0^1 \left\{g_{n_1, j} \circ g_{n_2, k}^{-1}(t) - g_{N_1, r_{1,j}} \circ g_{N_2, r_{2,k}}^{-1}(t)\right\}^+\ dt = 
\int_0^1 \left\{g_{n_1, j} \circ g_{n_2, k}^{-1}(t) - g_{N_1, r_{1,j}} \circ g_{N_2, r_{2,k}}^{-1}(t)\right\}\ dt.
$$
Thus,
\begin{align*}	
&\int_0^1 \left\{g_{n_1, j} \circ g_{n_2, k}^{-1}(t) - g_{N_1, r_{1,j}} \circ g_{N_2, r_{2,k}}^{-1}(t)\right\}^+\ dt \\
	&\qquad= \int_0^1 g_{n_1, j}(x) \ dg_{n_2, k}(x)- \int_0^1g_{N_1, r_{1,j}}(x) \ dg_{N_2, r_{2,k}}(x)\\
	&\qquad= \int_0^1 I_x(j, n_1 -j+1) \frac{x^{k-1}(1-x)^{n_2-k}}{B(k, n_2-k+1)} \ dx \\ 
	&\qquad\qquad- \int_0^1 I_x(r_{1,j}, N_1-r_{1,j}+1) \frac{x^{r_{2,k}-1}(1-x)^{N_2-r_{2,k}}}{B(k, N_2-k+1)} \ dx\\
	&\qquad =\int_0^1 \sum_{p=j}^{n_1}\left(\begin{array}{l}
n_1 \\
p
\end{array}\right) x^p(1-x)^{n_1-p} \frac{x^{k-1}(1-x)^{n_2-k}}{B(k, n_2-k+1)} \ dx \\
&\qquad\qquad - \int_0^1 \sum_{p=r_{1,j}}^{N_1}\left(\begin{array}{l}
N_1 \\
p
\end{array}\right) x^p(1-x)^{N_1-p} \frac{x^{r_{2,k}-1}(1-x)^{N_2-r_{2,k}}}{B(r_{2,k}, N_2-r_{2,k}+1)} \ dx\\ 
&\qquad = \sum_{p=j}^{n_1}\left(\begin{array}{l}
n_1 \\
p
\end{array}\right)\frac{B(k+p, n_2+n_1-p-k+1)}{B(j, n_2-k+1)} \\ 
&\qquad\qquad - \sum_{p=r_{1,j}}^{N_1}\left(\begin{array}{l}
N_1 \\
p
\end{array}\right)\frac{B(r_{2,k}+p, N_2+N_1-p-r_{2,k}+1)}{B(r_{2,k}, N_2-r_{2,k}+1)}.
\end{align*}
\end{proof}


Often it is of interest to compare the individuals with the highest aptitudes in their respective populations. Below we state the paradox probability in such setting using a Full House Model with two populations with the specification that $F_{Y_1} = F_Y$ for $i=1,2$.

\begin{corollary} \label{corollary:samedisttop}
Suppose that the conditions of Theorem~\ref{theorem:samedistgeneral} hold. Suppose that $1_1(X_{1,(N_1)}, \textbf{X}_{1,-(N_1)}) = 1_2(X_{2,(N_2)}, \textbf{X}_{2,-(N_2)}) = 1$. Then,
\begin{align*}
	&\Prob\left(X_{1,(N_1)} > X_{2,(N_2)}, Y_{1,(n_1)} < Y_{2,(n_2)}\right) 
  = \begin{cases}
      \frac{n_2}{n_1 + n_2} - \frac{N_2}{N_1 + N_2}, & \text{if}\ \frac{n_1}{N_1} \leq \frac{n_2}{N_2} \\
      0, & \text{otherwise}
    \end{cases}.
\end{align*}
\end{corollary}

A similar result holds for the individuals with the lowest latent aptitudes in the population.

\begin{corollary} \label{corollary:samedistbottom}
Suppose that the conditions of Theorem~\ref{theorem:samedistgeneral} hold. Suppose that $1_1(X_{1,(1)}, \textbf{X}_{1,-(1)}) = 1_2(X_{2,(1)}, \textbf{X}_{2,-(1)}) = 1$. Then,
\begin{align*}
	&\Prob\left(X_{1,(1)} > X_{2,(1)}, Y_{1,(1)} < Y_{2,(1)}\right) 
  = \begin{cases}
      \frac{N_2}{N_1 + N_2} - \frac{n_2}{n_1 + n_2}, & \text{if}\ \frac{n_1}{N_1} \geq \frac{n_2}{N_2} \\
      0, & \text{otherwise}
    \end{cases}.
\end{align*}
\end{corollary}


We now demonstrate a setting in which technical condition \eqref{eq:technical-condition} holds. This setting is based on a configuration of $n_j$, $N_j$, $j=1,2$ under the assumption that the systems are populated with individuals from the source populations who have the highest aptitudes.


\begin{prop} \label{prop:condition}
Consider the Full House Model in Definition~\ref{def:fhm} with two populations, where $F_{Y_i} = F_{Y}$ for $i=1,2$, and let the system inclusion function be $1_i(X_{i,j} \geq X_{i,(N_i - n_i + 1)})$. Denote by $I$ the regularized incomplete beta function. Suppose that $\frac{n_1}{n_2}\leq 1$ and $\frac{N_2}{N_1} \leq 1$, and set $k = j$. Under these assumptions, we conclude that technical condition \eqref{eq:technical-condition} holds and the conclusion of Theorem~\ref{theorem:samedistgeneral} applies.	
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}


Specification of $1_i(X_{i,j} \geq X_{i,(N_i - n_i + 1)})$ implies that $r_{i,j} = N_i + 1 - j$. 
\vspace{12pt}

When either $n_1 = N_1$ and $n_2 = N_2$ or $n_1 = n_2$ and $N_1 = N_2$ we have, for all $x \in (0,1)$, that
$$
  I_{N_2-j,j+1} \circ I_{N_1-j,j+1}^{-1} \circ I_{n_1-j,j+1} \circ I_{n_2-j,j+1}^{-1} (t) \geq t.
$$
The left hand side of the above equation increases whenever $n_1$ or $N_1$ are increased \citep{askitis2021logarithmic}, or $n_2$ or $N_1$ are decreased. Therefore, 
$
  I_{N_2-j,j+1} \circ I_{N_1-j,j+1}^{-1} \circ I_{n_1-j,j+1} \circ I_{n_2-j,j+1}^{-1} (t) \geq t
$
whenever $\frac{n_1}{n_2}\leq 1$ and $\frac{N_2}{N_1} \leq 1$. Observe that
\begin{align*}
&I_{N_2-j,j+1} \circ I_{N_1-j,j+1}^{-1} \circ I_{n_1-j,j+1} \circ I_{n_2-j,j+1}^{-1} (t) \geq t, \\ 
&\quad\implies I_{n_1-j,j+1} \circ I_{n_2-j,j+1}^{-1} (t) \geq I_{N_1-j,j+1} \circ I_{N_2-j,j+1}^{-1}(t),   \\
&\quad\implies I_{n_1-j,j+1} \circ I_{n_2-j,j+1}^{-1} (x) - I_{N_1-j,j+1} \circ I_{N_2-j,j+1}^{-1}(t) \geq t.
\end{align*}
Technical condition \eqref{eq:technical-condition} holds and the conclusion of Theorem~\ref{theorem:samedistgeneral} applies.
\end{proof}

\begin{defn}
We will say that $Y_2$ is first order $\varepsilon$-weakly stochastically dominant over $Y_1$ if, for $\varepsilon > 0$,

$
for all $y$ and 
$
  \Prob(Y_2 \leq y) < \Prob(Y_1 \leq y) \leq \Prob(Y_2 \leq y) + \varepsilon
$
for some $y$.
\end{defn}


\begin{prop} \label{prop:weakstodom}
Suppose that the system inclusion mechanism has the property that $g(X_{1,(r_{1,k})}) = 1$ and $g(X_{2,(r_{2,j})}) = 1$, and that the Full House Model is of the form 
\begin{align*}
  Y_{i,j} &\sim F_{Y_i}(y), \qquad i = 1,2, \qquad j = 1,\ldots,n_i, \\
  X_{i,j} &\sim F_X(x), \qquad i = 1,2, \qquad j = 1,\ldots,N_i, \\
\end{align*}
where $F_{Y_i}^{-1}(y)$ and $F_X^{-1}(x)$ exist. Further suppose that $Y_2$ is first order $\varepsilon$-weakly stochastically dominant over $Y_1$ for some $\varepsilon > 0$. Then,

\subsection{Recursive Full House Models}
\label{sec:rFHMsetup}

In this section we will investigate a simple recursive Full House Models in which Full House Models are repeatedly applied to individuals as they progress through iterations of some generic process. The general setup is that, in the first round, latent aptitude $X_{j} \overset{iid}{\sim} F_X$, $j = 1,\ldots,N$ will be generated  and then a label will be randomly assigned to each individual. There will be $m$ labels and each label will designate the outcome distribution. $F_{Y_{i,j}}$, $i = 1,\ldots,n$, $j = 1,\ldots,n_i$. Then outcomes will be realized as
\begin{equation} \label{eq:iteration}
  Y_{i,(r_{i,(j)})} = F_{Y_{i,j}}^{-1} \circ I_{r_{i,(j)},n_i-r_{i,(j)}+1}^{-1} \circ I_{j,N-j+1} \circ  F_X (X_{(j)}),	
\end{equation}
where $r_{i,(j)}$ is the rank of $X_{(j)}$ among the $n_i$ latent aptitudes also assigned label $i$. 

In the next iteration, all observations will be combined and then sorted in descending order by $Y_{i,(j)}$. The labels will be assigned in descending order, the largest $n_m$ values of $Y_{i,(j)}$ are assigned label $m$, the next largest $n_{m-1}$ values of $Y_{i,(j)}$ are assigned label $m-1$, and so on. We will setup these simulation so that $Y_i$ stochastically dominates $Y_{j}$ whenever $i > j$ so that higher outcomes are associated with higher labels. Then new $Y$ values are generated according to \eqref{eq:iteration}. The process will repeat until a pre-assigned stopping rule is satisfied. Note that this simulation setup assumes an immutable latent aptitude.


\subsubsection{Stop when highest latent aptitude is included in the highest-performing group}
\label{sec:rFHMbestgroup}

Here we consider a recursive Full House Modeling setup described in Section~\ref{sec:rFHMsetup} in which the recursion stops at the first iteration when $X_{(N)}$ is assigned label $m$. Here we specify that $N = 100,000$, $m=200$, $n_i = 500$, $i = 1,\ldots,m$. We will assume that $X_j \overset{iid}{\sim} \text{Pareto}(1.16)$, $j = 1,\ldots,N$. And we will assume that $Y_{i,j} \overset{iid}{\sim} N(\mu_i,\sigma_i)$, $j = 1,\ldots,n_i$ and $i = 1,\ldots,m$. We will specify that the $\mu_i$s as a set of 200 equally spaced values between 60 and 80 with $\mu_i$ increasing in $i$, ie $\mu_i = 60 - 20/199 + 20i/199$, $i=1,\ldots,m$. %These values will be assigned to the index $i$ in increasing order, meaning that $\mu_1$ will be the smallest value $(60)$ and $\mu_m$ will be the largest value $(80)$. 
We will fix $\sigma_i = \sigma$ and consider $\sigma \in \{0.5,1,2\}$. These simulation will be repeated 500 times.

To illustrate the procedure, consider a simplified scenario in which all $X_{(j)}$ values are realized at their median value. With this simplification, $Y_{i,(r_{i,(j)})}$ in \eqref{eq:iteration} is approximately $F_{Y_{i,j}}^{-1}( (r_{i,(j)} - 1/3)/(n_i + 1/3))$ \citep{kerman2011closed}. And we have that $Y_{i,(n_{i})} - Y_{i+1,(n_{i+1}-1)} > \mu_{i+1} - \mu_{i}$ for all considered values of $\sigma$. The individual with latent ability $X_{(N)}$ will consistently attain the value $Y_{i,(n_{i})}$, regardless their assigned group. Consequently, this individual's position among the $m$ groups will improve as the simulation advances. 

Results can be seen in Figure~\ref{Fig:rFHMrounds}. These Monte Carlo simulations reveal that it typically takes several rounds before the individual with the top aptitude belongs to the group that allows for the highest performances. On average, with $\sigma$ set to 0.5, 1, and 2, it takes 2.61, 4.19, and 7.46 rounds, respectively, for the individual with the top aptitude to join the highest-performing group (respective standard errors: 0.05, 0.09, 0.18).

These findings resonate with several empirical studies examining bias and distortion in real-world evaluative systems. The GRADE algorithm developed by \cite{waters2014grade} demonstrates how admission systems can bypass nuanced information (e.g., personal statements) in favor of machine-learned proxies, potentially reinforcing existing filters. Similarly, \cite{alvero2020ai} show that seemingly innocuous features (such as the word baseball) can serve as strong demographic signals. This raises concerns about indirect inference. A broader review by \cite{baker2022algorithmic} warns that algorithmic systems may harbor unknown unknowns, especially in global or under-examined subpopulations. Finally, the meta-analyses in \cite{walton2009latent} provide direct evidence that outcome-based measures (e.g., grades, test scores) can systematically understate the aptitude of marginalized groups due to identity-based threat.

These phenomena of latent talent being misclassified, sorted, and repeatedly filtered through context-specific outcome layers is precisely what this presentation of recursive Full House Modeling aims to formalize and study. At any stage in the selection process, you cannot be sure that the highest marks correspond to individuals with the highest ability. While the setting we consider is stylized and assumes fixed latent aptitude, it serves to illustrate how structural features of evaluative systems, not just randomness, can delay or obscure the recognition of true potential.

\subsection{Batting Averages in Major League Baseball}

We now apply Theorem~\ref{theorem:normalgeneral} and the reliability guarantees in Theorem~\ref{theorem:asymptotic} to evaluate the contextual extremeness of observed outcomes in a real-world system where the underlying distribution of aptitude is not directly observable and evolves over time. Major League Baseball (MLB) offers a canonical example of such a system. Here, player skill (latent batting aptitude) is inferred only indirectly through season-level observed outcomes (batting averages), which are shaped by changing rules, population structures, and environmental variance.

In particular, MLB introduced a series of rule changes in 2023 intended to increase in-game action, including a restriction on defensive positioning designed to raise batting averages on balls in play (BABIP) \citep{castrovince2023mlb}. 

First, the 2023 mean batting average for full-time players increased by 0.0057 (corresponding to an additional hit about every four baseball games) with a reduction in variability. A two-sample tests for differences in batting averages among full-time players from the 2023 and 2022 seasons has a p-value of 0.039. Thus, the rule changes seemed to be slightly effective in yielding increased batting averages\footnote{Technically, \cite{castrovince2023mlb} referred to MLB's desire to increased batting averages on balls in play (BABIP) while this analysis focused on batting averages. BABIP increased by 0.0061 from 2022 to 2023.} Moreover, the 2023 MLB batting leader Luis Arraez had a 0.354 batting average which was the first batting average to exceed .350 since Josh Hamilton in 2010. 

Now we turn to applying the results of Theorem~\ref{theorem:normalgeneral} in this example to contextualize the effect of these rule changes. Here we assume that seasonal batting averages of full-time players are normally distributed. The true means and standard deviations of these distributions are unknown. Thus, we estimate them via plug-in sample mean and sample standard-deviation and invoke the reliability guarantees from Theorem~\ref{theorem:asymptotic}. Figure~\ref{Fig:means-sd-full-time} presents smoothed estimates of the mean and standard deviation of batting averages for full-time players from 1871 to 2023. Notably, both the average and variability of batting averages in 2023 are among the lowest observed in the dataset, indicating a historically suppressed environment for batting averages of full-time batters.

Yet paradoxically, it appears modest when compared to higher averages in historically weaker environments. 

This demonstrates a central tenet of Full House Modeling: observed records are shaped as much by the structure of the evaluation system as by individual aptitude. Paradox probabilities offer a formal measure of this distortion. Without such contextualization, modern performances may be systematically undervalued. In particular, the modern structure of MLB makes it increasingly unlikely that even players with exceptional latent ability will produce historically dominant observed outcomes.

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:monotonicity}
Let $1 \leq j < n$ and let $x \in (0,1)$. Let $I_{\alpha, \beta}(x)$ be a regularized incomplete beta function as in Definition~\ref{def:ribf}. Then, $I_{n-j, j+1}(\xn) \geq I_{n-j+1, j+1}(\xnone)$.
\end{lemma}

	\textbf{Need to fix notation; should be $I_{\alpha,\beta}(x)$}
\end{center}

\begin{proof}
We can define a function $f_{n,j}(x)$ as
\begin{align*}
  f_{n,j}(x) & = I_{n-j, j+1}(\xn) - I_{n-j+1, j+1}(\xnone) \\
  &= \int_0^{\xn}\frac{y^{n-j-1}(1-y)^j}{B(n-j,j+1)}dy 
    - \int_0^{\xnone}\frac{y^{n-j}(1-y)^j}{B(n-j+1,j+1)}dy.
\end{align*}
An application of the chain rule yields 
\begin{align*}
  &f_{n,j}^\prime(x) = \frac{\left(\xn\right)^{n-j-1}\left(1-\xn\right)^j\left(x^{\frac{1-n}{n}}\right)}{nB(n-j,j+1)} 
    - \frac{\left(\xnone\right)^{n-j}\left(1-\xnone\right)^j\left(x^{-\frac{n}{n+1}}\right)}{(n+1)B(n-j+1,j+1)} \\
  &= \frac{\left(\xn\right)^{n-j-1}\left(1-\xn\right)^j\left(x^{\frac{1-n}{n}}\right)}{nB(n-j,j+1)} 
    - \frac{\left(\xnone\right)^{n-j}\left(1-\xnone\right)^j\left(x^{-\frac{n}{n+1}}\right)}{(n-j)B(n-j,j+1)} \\    
  &= \frac{1}{B(n-j,j+1)}\left(\frac{\left(\xn\right)^{n-j-1}\left(1-\xn\right)^j\left(x^{\frac{1-n}{n}}\right)}{n} 
    - \frac{\left(\xnone\right)^{n-j}\left(1-\xnone\right)^j\left(x^{-\frac{n}{n+1}}\right)}{n-j}\right),
\end{align*}
where we used $B(n-j+1,j+1) = \frac{n-j}{n+1}B(n-j,j+1)$ in the second equality. Now define $A_{n,j} = \{x:f_{n,j}^\prime(x) \geq 0\}$. For $x \in A_{n,j}$ we have
\begin{align*}
	&\frac{\left(\xn\right)^{n-j-1}\left(1-\xn\right)^j\left(x^{\frac{1-n}{n}}\right)}{n} 
    \geq \frac{\left(\xnone\right)^{n-j}\left(1-\xnone\right)^j\left(x^{-\frac{n}{n+1}}\right)}{n-j} \\
    &\qquad\implies \frac{\left(\xn\right)^{n-j-1}\left(1-\xn\right)^j\left(x^{\frac{1-n}{n}}\right)}{\left(\xnone\right)^{n-j}\left(1-\xnone\right)^j\left(x^{-\frac{n}{n+1}}\right)} \geq \frac{n}{n-j} \\
    &\qquad\implies x^{-\frac{j}{n(n+1)}}\left(\frac{1-\xn}{1-\xnone}\right)^j \geq \frac{n}{n-j} \\
    &\qquad\implies \frac{x^{-\frac{1}{n(n+1)}}-\xnone}{1-\xnone} \geq \left(\frac{n}{n-j}\right)^\frac{1}{j}.
\end{align*}
Define $g_n(x) = \frac{x^{-\frac{1}{n(n+1)}}-\xnone}{1-\xnone}$. Then 
\begin{align*}
	g_n(x) = \frac{x^{-\frac{1}{n(n+1)}}-\xnone}{1-\xnone} = \frac{x^{-\frac{1}{n(n+1)}} - 1}{1-\xnone} + 1,
\end{align*}
and
\begin{align*}
	g^{'}_n(x) &= \frac{1}{(1-\xnone)^2}\left(-\frac{1}{n(n+1)}x^{-\frac{1}{n(n+1)}-1}(1-\xnone) - (x^{-\frac{1}{n(n+1)}} - 1)(-\frac{1}{n+1}x^{\frac{1}{n+1}-1})\right)\\
	&= \frac{1}{(1-\xnone)^2}\left( \frac{1}{n}x^{-\frac{n^2+1}{n(n+1)}} - \frac{1}{n+1}x^{-\frac{n}{n+1}} - \frac{1}{n(n+1)}x^{-\frac{n^2 + n + 1}{n(n+1)}}\right)\\
	&= \frac{x^{-\frac{n^2+1}{n(n+1)}}}{n(n+1)(1-\xnone)^2}\left((n+1) - nx^{\frac{1}{n(n+1)}} - x^{-\frac{1}{n+1}}\right).
\end{align*}
Now define $l_n(x) = (n+1) - nx^{\frac{1}{n(n+1)}} - x^{-\frac{1}{n+1}}$ and see that
\begin{align*}
	l^{'}_n(x) = -\frac{1}{n+1}x^{\frac{1}{n(n+1)} - 1} + \frac{1}{n+1}x^{-\frac{1}{n+1} - 1} = \frac{1}{n+1}x^{-\frac{n+2}{n+1}}\left( 1 - \xn \right).
\end{align*}
Thus, $l_n(x)$ is an increasing function for $x \in (0,1)$ and $\sup_{x \in (0,1)}l_n(x) = 0$. Therefore, $l_n(x) < 0$ for $x \in (0,1)$. Thus, $g_n(x)$ is an decreasing function, and $g_n(x)\to1$ as $x\to1$. We can see that $g_n(x) \in (1,+\infty)$ for $x \in (0,1)$. Thus $A_{n,j} = (0,x^*_{n,j}]$ where $x^*_{n,j}$ is such that $g_n(x^*_{n,j}) = (\frac{n}{n-j})^j$. Putting it all together, $f_{n,j}(0) = f_{n,j}(1) = 0$ and this implies that $I_{n-j, j+1}(\xn) \geq I_{n-j+1, j+1}(\xnone)$ for all $x \in (0,1)$.
\end{proof}

%Given the property of the system inclusion mechanism, $g(X_{i,j},\textbf{X}_{i,-j}) = I(X_{i,j} \geq X_{N_i-n_i+1})$, and the condtion $k=j$, we have
%$$f(x) = g_{n_1, n_1-j} \circ g_{n_2, n_2-j}^{-1}(x) - g_{N_1, N_1 - j} \circ g_{N_2, N_2-j}^{-1}(x)$$
The proof will use two induction arguments. For the base case let $n_1 = n_2$ and $N_1 = N_2$. With these specifications we have that 
$$
  g_{n_1, n_1-j} \circ g_{n_2, n_2-j}^{-1}(x) - g_{N_1, N_1 - j} \circ g_{N_2, N_2-j}^{-1}(x) = 0.
$$
For the induction step, choose some $m \leq n_1 - 1$ and suppose that 
\begin{equation} \label{eq:inductstep}
  g_{n_1 -m, n_1-m -j} \circ g_{n_2, n_2-j}^{-1}(t) - g_{N_1, N_1 - j} \circ g_{N_2, N_2 - j}^{-1}(t) \geq 0,	
\end{equation}
for $t \in (0,1)$ where $n_2 \geq n_1$ and $N_1 \geq N_2$. An integration by parts calculation applied to regularized incomplete beta functions gives
$$
  I_{\alpha,\beta}(x) = I_{\alpha+1,\beta}(x) + \frac{x^\alpha(1-x)^\beta}{\alpha B(\alpha,\beta)}.
$$
Applying the above to \eqref{eq:inductstep} yields
\begin{align*}
	&g_{n_1-m-1, n_1-m-1-j} \circ g_{n_2, n_2-j}^{-1}(t) - g_{N_1, N_1 - j} \circ g_{N_2, N_2 - j}^{-1}(t) \\ 
	&\qquad= g_{n_1-m, n_1-m-j} \circ g_{n_2, n_2-j}^{-1}(t) - g_{N_1, N_1 -j} \circ g_{N_2, N_2-j}^{-1}(t) \\
	&\qquad\qquad+ \frac{\left[g_{n_2, n_2-j}^{-1}(t)\right]^{n_1-m-1-j}\left[1 - g_{n_2, n_2-j}^{-1}(t)\right]^{j+1}}{(n_1-m-1-j)B(n_1-m-1-j,j+1)} \\
	&\qquad\geq 0.
\end{align*}
A similar argument holds for iterative increases to $N_1$. Thus, condition~\eqref{eq:technical-condition} holds under are assumed conditions and the conclusions of Theorem~\ref{theorem:samedistgeneral} follow.
\end{proof}
